A corridor operator is not regulated once. You are regulated at every point you touch: where you take money in, where the stablecoin is issued, where you pay money out, and by whoever supervises the correspondent relationships in between. Four regimes matter for EU↔Africa, and they are converging on the same structural shape from very different starting points. Understanding each one is not optional background reading — it determines which stablecoins you can settle in, what your onboarding obligations are, and whether your compliance programme will survive an examination.

**Not legal advice, and time-sensitive.** This page reflects the regulatory picture as of **August 2026**, and rulemaking is live in several of these jurisdictions. ## United States — the GENIUS Act

The **Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act** ([S.1582, 119th Congress](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1582/text)) was enacted in July 2025 and is the first comprehensive federal framework for payment stablecoins. Because roughly 99% of stablecoin supply is dollar-denominated, this regime shapes the asset almost every corridor settles in — whether or not your corridor touches the United States.

The statute is short on operational detail and long on delegation, so most of 2026 has been rulemaking. Tracking the rules matters more than reading the Act.

  </Step>

  </Step>
</Steps>

The structural feature worth internalising: an issuer may operate under a qualifying **state** regime until its outstanding stablecoins exceed **$10 billion**, at which point — absent a waiver — it must transition into the federal regime or stop issuing. That threshold is the hinge of the whole design. It permits genuine experimentation at small scale while pulling anything systemically relevant under federal supervision.

**For a payments business, the practical consequence is narrow and important: which stablecoin you settle in is now a regulatory decision, not just a liquidity one.** The issuer's regime, reserve rules, and redemption obligations become part of your own risk surface — you inherit the credit quality of whoever you settle through.

## European Union — MiCA

[Regulation (EU) 2023/1114](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1114/oj) became fully applicable on 30 December 2024. It splits stablecoins into two categories, and the distinction is not cosmetic.

| | E-money tokens (Title IV) | Asset-referenced tokens (Title III) |
|---|---|---|
| References | A single official currency | A basket, commodities, or a mix |
| Issuer must be | A credit institution or an authorised EMI under Directive 2009/110/EC | An authorised ART issuer |
| Redemption | At par, at any time, at the holder's request | More complex, and weaker |
| Practical read | The category a EUR or USD stablecoin used in payments falls into | Everything else |

**EMTs face the strictest reserve and redemption rules**, and that is deliberate. MiCA treats a single-currency payment token as e-money and regulates it accordingly rather than inventing a lighter-touch category for it.

**1 July 2026 was the end of the EU-wide transitional period.** Entities providing crypto-asset services in the EU without MiCA authorisation can no longer rely on grandfathering arrangements. In practice, this is the date the EU side of a corridor stopped being a grey area — either you have authorisation, or you stop.

<Note>
Arc's EUR leg is the leg that would sit under this regime. The architecture reflects it in one specific way: verification level is a hard gate on what a customer may do, with limits expressed in a single reference currency — because a limits regime that varies per corridor is a limits regime nobody can audit.
</Note>

## Kenya — the Virtual Asset Service Providers Act, 2025

Kenya moved from having essentially no framework to having a fairly complete one in under a year. The [VASP Act, 2025](https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/act/2025/20/eng@2025-11-04) received presidential assent on 15 October 2025, and the implementing regulations were gazetted in July 2026, completing the framework.

Its notable feature is a **dual-regulator architecture** that follows function rather than technology:

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Central Bank of Kenya" icon="building-columns">
    Custodial wallet providers, virtual asset payment processors, and stablecoin issuance. This is the regulator that binds for a corridor paying out to M-Pesa.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Capital Markets Authority" icon="chart-line">
    Exchanges and trading platforms. A payment processor and a trading venue pose different risks and belong with different supervisors.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

For a corridor operator paying out to M-Pesa, the CBK side is the one that binds — Arc's KES payout leg is a payment-processing activity, not a trading one. The dual-regulator split is the right instinct and worth understanding before you design your entity structure.

## Nigeria — ISA 2025 and after

The **Investments and Securities Act 2025** gives Nigeria's SEC express jurisdiction over virtual and digital asset exchanges, custodians, and VASPs, replacing a period in which the central bank and the SEC had visibly different postures on the same activity. The Act includes enforcement machinery — notably a "National Confiscation Wallet" for seized assets — which signals an intent to enforce, not just to categorise.

Alongside it, the **Nigeria Tax Administration Act 2025** confirms a 10% capital gains charge on the disposal of digital assets, and clarifies that not every virtual asset is necessarily an SEC-regulated security.

<Warning>
Nigeria is also the jurisdiction where the gap between regulation and behaviour is widest. Chainalysis puts Nigerian on-chain value received at roughly **$92 billion** for the year to June 2025 — the largest in the region — driven substantially by inflation and foreign-currency access rather than anything the current framework explicitly contemplates. You need to build for the flows that exist, under the rules as written. Those are not the same specification, and the gap between them is real compliance risk.
</Warning>

## The pattern across all four regimes

Read the four together and the convergence is clear. Every regime is arriving at the same four requirements from different starting points.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Reserve quality and segregation" icon="vault">
    Short-dated, high-quality assets, bankruptcy-remote from the issuer. The maturity transformation that caused previous failures is being legislated away in every jurisdiction.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Redemption at par, on demand" icon="right-left">
    The promise made explicit and legally enforceable, rather than left to terms of service that can be amended overnight.
  </Card>
  <Card title="AML and sanctions as a programme" icon="shield">
    Not a screening step bolted on. A programme with governance, testing, and records — which is why Arc treats compliance as a hard pre-condition rather than a side channel.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Licensed entities at both ends" icon="id-card">
    The permissionless middle leg does not make the endpoints permissionless. Someone regulated takes the money in and someone regulated pays it out.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

That last point is the one most often missed when people describe stablecoin corridors as disintermediating. **The chain replaces the correspondent banks in the middle. It does not replace the licensed entity at either end.** Arc's architecture encodes exactly this shape: an unverified account can hold a virtual account and receive funds but cannot send, and no transfer leaves the compliance gate without a recorded decision.
